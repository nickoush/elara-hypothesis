# The Elara Hypothesis

## Theia and Her History

---

### Author's note

This began as a scene in a novel.

I was writing *Elara — The First of You* and wanted the Moon's existence to carry more than decorative weight — to make it hinge on something so improbable that its mere presence in the sky would seem astonishing. I like hard science fiction, the kind you could defend in front of someone who actually knows. So I started reading about the Moon's origin, and ended up much deeper in than I had planned.

A disclaimer is in order: I am not a scientist. I wandered into territory that isn't mine, and it's likely that somewhere along the way I've said something foolish. If a specialist reads this, I ask for patience and thank them in advance for any correction.

Two things caught my attention while I was reading.

The first is that every hypothesis about the Moon **begins** with Theia striking Earth. Theia enters the story already dying. We know her for an instant and she vanishes, like a spark. And yet she was a world: Mars-sized, and she existed for something like a hundred million years — a span comparable to the entire reign of the dinosaurs. It seemed strange to me that a body like that would have left no other trace. I started looking at the neighbouring planets.

The second is that planet-formation models work well up to a point and then stop working. They reproduce the masses of Venus and Earth without difficulty. But from Earth outward the anomalies pile up: a Mars that ought to be five or ten times larger than it is, an asteroid belt with a thousand times less mass than it should have, and in between, us, with a wildly disproportionate satellite. That stretch — Earth to Jupiter — is where physics stops explaining everything on its own.

And those are not the only oddities. Mars looks split down the middle. One hemisphere is a smooth, sunken plain almost free of craters; the other is high, rough, battered terrain. Between them runs an escarpment kilometres high. This is not a difference of shading: they are two surfaces that look like they belong to different planets, joined along a seam.

And then there is the coincidence that finally hooked me.

Both happened in the same era. The Moon-forming impact is placed between 4,510 and 4,430 million years ago. The Martian dichotomy sits roughly there as well. I am not the first to notice: Margarita Marinova, author of the impact model that best reproduces Borealis, mentioned it in her 2008 paper — and attributed it, prudently, to the fact that impacts of that size only occurred very early in Solar System history.

That is the sensible reading. Same era, same population of giant projectiles, no connection between the two events.

But the question stays with you.

**What happened 4,500 million years ago?**

That is where the following idea came from. The rule I followed was a single one: **the most probable explanation is usually the simplest one.** Not three or four rare coincidences chained together, but one coincidence that triggered three consequences.

This is the Elara hypothesis. It is about Earth's abnormally large Moon, about a Mars split down the middle and abnormally small, and about the disappearance of a planet that appears here not as a passing stranger but as the neighbour we had for a hundred million years.

---

## The small Mars problem

There is a problem in Solar System formation that rarely gets mentioned outside conferences: **Mars should not be this small**.

Simulations of how the rocky planets assembled work reasonably well. They reproduce Venus. They reproduce Earth. But when they get to Mars they systematically produce a planet of roughly Earth's mass — five to ten times larger than the real one. Planetary embryos in that region **merge**. That is the default outcome, the one that comes out again and again.

Our Mars, at one tenth of Earth's mass, is the exception that needs explaining. The field has a name for it: the small Mars problem.

And there is another fact usually told as a cosmic accident: Earth has a disproportionate Moon, the product of an impact with a body called Theia. But giant impacts are not accidents either. They are the expected endgame of planet formation; every simulation produces several. What is anomalous about the Moon-forming impact is not that it happened, but how finely tuned it turned out to be: near-minimum velocity, favourable angle, and an impactor isotopically almost identical to Earth.

Two oddities, in the same era, on two neighbouring planets.

This is the hypothesis that they were the same thing.

---

## The idea, in four sentences

Theia had a moon of her own.

The system passed close to Mars. The moon struck the planet and excavated Borealis, the enormous basin that divides Mars into two distinct hemispheres.

Theia survived and continued on her way.

Millions of years later she collided with the proto-Earth, and from that collision came our Moon.

Read this way, Mars did not avoid merging with a planetary embryo by chance: it avoided it **barely**, and the scar of that near-miss is the largest geological feature on its surface. The embryo carried on and ended up in us.

---

## The deficit that moved house

There is a consequence worth looking at separately, because it changes how the small Mars problem reads.

Theia and Mars shared a region. Isotopic models place Theia in the inner Solar System, and the velocity constraint imposed by Borealis — discussed below — forces her onto an orbit with aphelion at Mars. For something like a hundred million years, between one and one and a half astronomical units, there were **two Mars-mass bodies**, not one.

The arithmetic is simple:

| | Earth masses |
|---|---|
| present-day Mars | 0.107 |
| Theia | 0.100 |
| **total before the exodus** | **0.207** |

**Forty-eight percent of all the mass in the Martian region ended up in Earth.**

It is tempting to overstate what that buys, so: models predict between 0.5 and 1.0 Earth masses for that zone. Mars alone falls short by a factor of five to nine; Mars plus Theia fall short by a factor of two to five.

In other words, **the hypothesis halves the deficit. It does not remove it.**

But it reframes the problem, and that is the interesting part. The deficit stops being purely a failure of accretion — material that never came together — and becomes, in part, a **transfer**: material that did come together, into a planet-sized body, and then moved inward. The region is not half-empty because there was nothing to fill it with. It is half-empty because half of it left.

And on the other side of the ledger, that migration is ten percent of Earth's mass.

---

## What the system would have looked like

What follows are the numbers that come out of simulating sixty thousand possible encounters with Mars and keeping the ones that reproduce Borealis with the correct geometry. One hundred and seventy-five survive — 0.29 %.

**Theia** was essentially a twin of Mars: one tenth of an Earth mass, roughly 3,400 km in radius.

**Her moon** was about 2,000 km across, slightly smaller than Pluto, with 2 % of Mars's mass. The mass ratio between them was 1 to 47 — between the Earth–Moon system (1 to 81) and Pluto–Charon (1 to 8). Nothing exotic.

**It orbited at 28,500 km**, about eight times Theia's radius, completing one revolution every **41.8 hours**. At that distance it would have become tidally locked within a few million years: it always showed Theia the same face, as ours does to us.

**The encounter lasted a little over a week.** Theia took 7.6 days to cross Mars's gravitational sphere of influence and passed 28,500 km from the planet's centre — about eight Martian radii. Her moon struck **2.4 hours before** Theia reached closest approach.

The moon hit at **6.5 km/s at an angle of 41 to 45 degrees** from the surface.

That number deserves a pause. Published models of Borealis require between 30 and 60 degrees to reproduce the basin's size and elliptical shape. And 45 degrees is, statistically, **the most probable angle at which one body strikes another**. The scenario doesn't satisfy the condition by tuning or by luck: it produces the distribution you would expect, and the published window covers its central portion.

---

## A property of Theia we did not put there

Of everything the simulations could say, this is what I least expected to find.

The orientation of the satellite's orbital plane around Theia was sampled at random, isotropically: every inclination was equally likely at the outset. It was not data, it was noise. But looking at which of those sixty thousand configurations survived the Borealis filters, something showed up.

| binary obliquity | observed | expected |
|---|---|---|
| near-coplanar (either sense) | **49.1 %** | 33.3 % |
| intermediate | 28.0 % | 33.3 % |
| polar | **22.9 %** | 33.3 % |

Binaries coplanar with Theia's orbit are **more than twice as common** as polar ones among compatible cases. The mean of cos²(obliquity) is 0.459 against 0.333 expected under isotropy, a 5.6-sigma result, confirmed by an independent two-sample test against the full sampled distribution.

The mechanism turned out to be simple. The satellite's orbital velocity around Theia was almost 1.2 km/s — 36 % of the speed at which the system approached Mars. If at the moment of encounter the satellite was moving **with** that approach, the two velocities add and the impact reaches the 6 km/s Borealis requires. If it was moving against, the blow falls below the minimum and the basin does not form.

For the satellite ever to be aligned, though, its orbital plane must **contain the approach direction**. A perpendicular plane never manages it, at any phase of the orbit. And since the approach direction lies close to Theia's orbital plane, coplanar binaries are selected.

One detail confirms the explanation: the selection **does not distinguish prograde from retrograde**. The sense of the satellite's motion is irrelevant, because the condition is on the plane, not the direction. The test looking for a prograde preference returns null, exactly as it should.

From this follows an inference about Theia, with two conditionals worth keeping in plain sight. **If** the hypothesis holds, and **if** that satellite formed from an earlier impact — as the scenario assumes — then the debris disc that produced it and the spin that same impact gave Theia shared an axis. Under both assumptions:

> **Theia's spin axis would have been roughly perpendicular to her orbit.** Obliquity near zero or near 180 degrees; the analysis cannot distinguish between them.

It is as close as one can get to saying something about how a planet nobody ever saw was turning. And it must be said with the label attached: **inferred**, not measured.

---

## What it did to Mars

**The orbit: little, and without memory.** Theia's flyby and the impact together changed Mars's orbital velocity by about 4 %, and since Theia had nearly equal mass, the flyby mattered more than the collision. But none of it survives: the inner Solar System is chaotically unstable, and no present-day orbital element of Mars retains any memory of an event 4.5 billion years ago.

**The rotation: everything.** This is the largest effect and the least explored. The impact delivered 2.59 × 10³² kg·m²/s of angular momentum to Mars. The planet's current spin holds 1.91 × 10³². **The blow supplied 36 % more than Mars has today in rotation.** Its day was not perturbed by the event — it was reconfigured.

**The crust: the asymmetry we see.** The impact removed crust in one hemisphere and deposited ejecta over the other. And an independent observation fits: Mars's magnetic sources lie about 9 km deep in the north and about 32 km deep in the south. It has been proposed that the deep southern layer represents ancient terrain, predating the dichotomy, buried beneath material thrown out by the basin itself.

**The magnetic field: we don't know.** It would be attractive to say the impact switched off the Martian dynamo. The data don't allow it. There is evidence of a magnetic field both before and after several of the large basins, and estimates for the final shutdown fall hundreds of millions of years later. The honest statement is that the impact altered the planet's thermal evolution, and that its role in the dynamo's demise remains open.

**The present-day moons.** Debris from the impact would have formed a disc around Mars, from which the precursors of Phobos and Deimos emerged. With one detail that is often told wrong: in a giant impact the disc is made **mostly of the struck planet**. Phobos and Deimos are, almost entirely, Mars. What Theia's moon contributed is a trace — and that trace is precisely what makes the whole story testable.

---

## What the encounter did not do

Here is an important correction, and it came from checking an intuition that seemed obvious.

It's tempting to tell the story this way: the encounter with Mars deflected Theia and set her on course for Earth. It sounds good and it's what you'd expect.

It's false.

The perturbation Theia receives is 4 to 6 % of her velocity. That's enough for a great deal, but not enough to move a perihelion from 1.34 astronomical units to 0.96. And the numbers confirm it fairly decisively:

| | fraction of orbits crossing Earth's |
|---|---|
| **before** the encounter | **26.7 %** |
| **after** the encounter | **21.7 %** |

The orbits capable of reaching Earth **already existed before Borealis**. They were in fact slightly more common; the encounter reduced them a little.

What decides everything is the direction from which Theia arrived at Mars. Those approaching from behind, subtracting orbital velocity from the planet, end up in tighter orbits with perihelia of 0.90–0.96 AU: already Earth-crossing. Those arriving from ahead end up in wide orbits that only touch Mars, with perihelia of 1.3 to 1.5.

So the encounter opened no new path. **It chose which of the already available paths would be taken.**

That is less dramatic than the intuitive version, but it has an advantage: the hypothesis no longer needs the encounter to do anything dynamically improbable. It only needs Theia to have been where she was.

---

## Mars versus Earth

With the trajectory already fixed beforehand, a natural question remains: what was Theia's most likely fate?

The simulation answers it by measuring both things at once, with the same physics and the same detection quality, and accounting for the fact that they compete — once Theia falls into one planet, she can no longer fall into the other.

| population | cases | expected impacts with Mars | with Earth | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cross Earth's orbit | 38 | 0.35 | 4.15 | 0.09 |
| **do not cross it** | **137** | **2.84** | **0.06** | **50 : 1** |
| total | 175 | 3.19 | 4.20 | 0.76 |

In **78 % of the encounter's possible outcomes, Mars wins fifty to one**. That is the normal destination. Only in the remaining 22 % does Earth even enter the competition.

And on the total, the ratio of 0.76 is not distinguishable from one: the two figures rest on very different statistics and the margin of error is around 0.25. It's a tie.

Which raises something we didn't expect. If Theia spends far more time near Mars, how does Earth manage to draw even?

> Collision cross-section of the proto-Earth: 1.24 × 10¹⁵ m²
> Collision cross-section of Mars: 2.87 × 10¹⁴ m²

**Earth is 4.3 times larger as a target.** Its escape velocity — 9.14 km/s against 4.96 — means its gravity pulls in trajectories that would otherwise pass by. Theia has many more encounters with Mars, but each Earth encounter is worth four times as much.

That balances the scales. The fate of a body that shaped half of Martian geology and all of our Moon was decided, essentially, by a coin flip.

---

## Afterwards

The interval to the Moon-forming impact comes out of the simulation: between a hundred thousand and ten million years, with most of the probability concentrated in the first few million. The decay of that rate with time was measured, and it falls to less than half between two hundred thousand and two million years — so the effective window is shorter than a naive extrapolation would suggest.

And when Theia arrived, she arrived well. **Half of the possible impacts simultaneously satisfy the four conditions** that lunar formation requires: angular momentum compatible with the present Earth–Moon system, velocity in the right regime, bounded final eccentricity, and a final terrestrial orbit matching the one we have.

That last point has a pleasing consequence. The impact pushes Earth's orbit outward by 1.8 %, because Theia contributes angular momentum. To end up at one astronomical unit, the proto-Earth had to have been at roughly **0.983**. We were slightly closer to the Sun.

---

## And now the uncomfortable part

Everything above describes how it would have been if it had happened.

The probability that it actually happened is **around one percent**, with an honest range from 0.1 to 10.

It's worth understanding where that number comes from, because it isn't where you'd expect.

Of the five links in the chain, four can be simulated, and **all four came out favourably**. The Borealis geometry emerges on its own. Theia survives the encounter in one hundred percent of cases where her moon impacts. She reaches Earth with a 7 % probability over ten million years — comparable to that of any planetary embryo, which runs between 4 and 8 %. And the lunar impact geometry is correct half the time.

The link everyone assumed was fragile — how Theia travels from Mars to us — turned out to be the solid one.

What costs is the premise: **that the Borealis projectile arrived accompanied**. That it was another body's moon rather than a free-floating planetesimal. That factor is unmeasured, has no dedicated literature, and absorbs nearly all of the penalty.

The other two remaining links don't depend on dynamics either. One is the age of Borealis, estimated only indirectly while the other large basins have firm dates. The other is where Theia came from: a 2025 study places her in the inner Solar System, which the hypothesis requires, but another line of research holds that she may have been a carbonaceous object arriving from beyond Jupiter, which would kill it. That debate is open right now.

---

## What was normal, and didn't happen

Here, I think, is what makes all of this worth telling.

Suppose Theia had struck Mars instead of passing by. This is not a far-fetched fantasy: **it is the default outcome**. It is what the simulations produce most of the time, and it is why the small Mars problem remains unsolved.

The numbers for that world can be calculated.

Mars would have gone from 0.107 to **0.207 Earth masses**. Double. A radius of 4,050 km instead of 3,390, gravity of 5.03 m/s² instead of 3.71, escape velocity of 6.4 km/s instead of 5.0.

It would not be an Earth. It would still be nearly five times less massive, and at 1.52 astronomical units it would receive 43 % of the light we do. It would be a **super-Mars**: an intermediate world.

But geologically it would be another story. Mars died mainly from being small: small planets cool fast, lose their volcanism, stop replenishing their atmosphere. Doubling its mass extends that lifetime substantially. Its wet period, which in reality lasted hundreds of millions of years, would probably have lasted **billions**.

And it would have a large moon of its own. The mass ratio between Theia and Mars is 0.93, almost one to one — far more even than the one-to-nine of the actual lunar impact. A collision like that, at low velocity, produces an enormous disc.

Meanwhile Earth would have stayed at 0.9 Earth masses, with no Moon. It would still be a temperate planet with oceans and an atmosphere: the mass and the distance are right. But its axis would be considerably less stable, its day would run eight to twelve hours instead of twenty-four, and its tides would be solar only, a third of the present ones. Intertidal zones — where many place decisive steps in life's move onto land — would be far narrower.

Two medium planets, one with a large moon and one with a small moon or none. A more even system, duller, closer to what the models predict.

---

## How it can be tested

A hypothesis that cannot be refuted is worth nothing. This one can be refuted four different ways.

**1. The chronology.** Borealis must predate the Moon-forming impact. No exceptions. A reliable date showing the reverse order kills the hypothesis outright — and as it happens, the age of the Martian dichotomy is the worst-determined number in the whole scenario: it is estimated only indirectly, while the other large basins have firm dates.

**2. The isotopic signature in Mars's moons.** This is the principal test, and it's interesting because the scenario imposes it on itself.

An impact-generated satellite forms from its parent body's mantle. So Theia's moon was isotopically **Theia**, not merely similar. And we know Theia indirectly, through the Earth–Moon system, which we have been measuring for sixty years.

That means the exogenous component preserved in the Martian system should match the composition attributable to Theia within analytical error. Not resemble it: match it. If the two signatures come out clearly different, the hypothesis falls even if both point to the same region of the Solar System.

JAXA's MMX mission will return samples from Phobos in 2031, with Deimos flybys — and Deimos, by virtue of its orbit, is the older object in the Martian system and the preferred archive.

**3. A Martian mantle domain shifted toward Earth.** This test requires no mission at all.

If the impactor left part of its material in Mars's mantle, it left its signature too. And there is a new reason to think that signature could have survived: in 2026 the Martian meteorite NWA 13441 was found to carry a neodymium isotopic composition corresponding to the initial Solar System, indicating that **a portion of the Martian mantle remained unaltered for billions of years**. Mars, lacking plate tectonics, preserves what Earth erased.

If the Martian mantle holds unhomogenised domains, the projectile's material didn't dilute away — it stayed in one of them. The prediction is therefore that some Martian meteorite should sample a domain with oxygen, chromium or titanium isotopes **shifted toward terrestrial values** relative to the Martian average, because Theia was, isotopically, nearly Earth.

There are four hundred Martian meteorites in laboratories worldwide. The measurement could be made tomorrow. The honest difficulty is magnitude: with an impactor at 2 % of Mars's mass, the shift would be small and could be confused with primordial heterogeneity.

**4. Deimos and Phobos must agree with each other.** Both come from the same disc. If they carry different exogenous populations, something is wrong with the scenario.

---

## Us

What we have is a tiny dead Mars, and an Earth with a disproportionate satellite that stabilises its axis, slows its rotation and raises tides of metres.

If the hypothesis were true, those two things would be **the same fact seen from two sides**. A planetary embryo that was already on the right orbit passed close to Mars, lost its moon against it, and carried on along the path it was already travelling.

Mars kept the scar. We kept the embryo — literally: ten percent of this planet's mass came from over there.

And the hypothesis need not be true for the underlying observation to hold: **what we see when we look at the sky is the result of the improbable branch**. Normal was a large Mars. Normal was a small moon, or none. Normal was a system that produces neither Earth's axial stability nor the tides that shaped its coastlines.

One percent, or something like it, separates this world from all the ones that could have been.

And somewhere in the middle of all that there was a planet nobody ever saw, which lasted a hundred million years, and of which exactly two things remain — if this is right: a basin the size of a hemisphere on a dead world, and the light that comes through the window on clear nights.

---

*This is the Elara hypothesis: it describes a proposal, not an established result.*

## Mission

- **MMX (Martian Moons eXploration)**, JAXA. Sample return from Phobos with Deimos flybys; samples due on Earth in 2031. Stated objectives include determining the origin of Phobos and Deimos.

## Simulation software

All orbital calculations reported here were carried out with **REBOUND**:

- Rein, H. & Liu, S.-F. (2012). *REBOUND: an open-source multi-purpose N-body code for collisional dynamics.* **Astronomy & Astrophysics** 537, A128.
- Rein, H. & Spiegel, D. S. (2015). *IAS15: a fast, adaptive, high-order integrator for gravitational dynamics.* **MNRAS** 446, 1424.
- Rein, H. et al. (2019). *Hybrid symplectic integrators for planetary dynamics.* **MNRAS** 485, 5490. — The MERCURIUS integrator.
